This ran in *Computers and Automation* in June 1968. A Minneapolis company called Data International, offering to lease you your software. No capital investment. The cost spread out "over the product lifetime of the computer system."

Swap the drawing for a stock photo and it would run today. No upfront cost. A predictable monthly payment. Keep your cash for the business.

## In 1968 it made sense

Software then was written by hand, for one machine that filled a room. Almost no business could pay for that outright. Renting was the only way in.

## The reason went away. The rent didn't.

For a while businesses did own their software. A boxed copy of the accounting program sat on a shelf and ran for years. Then subscriptions came back, mostly because they were easy to sell and easy to buy. Nobody went back and asked whether the 1968 reason still held.

It doesn't. Software built around one business, and handed over to that business, costs a fraction of what it once did. For a lot of small businesses, a few years of subscriptions now [pays for a system they keep](/writing/what-your-software-costs/).

## Worth asking

For each tool you pay for every month: if the company behind it disappeared tomorrow, what would you still have? Your customer list, your job history, your prices, your website?

If the answer is "not much," that's the part of the business you're renting. Some of it is fine to rent. The parts the business runs on usually aren't. That's the work I do: [one system the business owns](/help/custom-software/), in place of the stack.
