Nobody signs up for $860 a month of software. You sign up for a $39 website builder. Then a scheduling app, because the phone was getting out of hand. Accounting, because the accountant asked. A texting tool, because a salesperson called. Each one is a reasonable decision. Nobody adds them up.

Here's a typical stack for a small service business, at list prices checked in October 2026:

| Tool | Monthly | Five years |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduling app | $189 | $11,340 |
| Accounting | $85 | $5,100 |
| Website builder | $39 | $2,340 |
| Reviews and texting | $399 | $23,940 |
| Online booking | $49 | $2,940 |
| Business phone line | $19 | $1,140 |
| Email marketing | $20 | $1,200 |
| CRM, three seats | $60 | $3,600 |
| **Total** | **$860** | **$51,600** |

The reviews-and-texting figure is a third-party estimate. Those platforms don't publish prices.

That five-year column assumes nothing goes up. Things go up. Seats get added as you hire. A feature you rely on moves to the next tier.

## And then the hours

Software that doesn't talk to other software turns into typing. A customer's name goes into the scheduling app, then the invoice, then the books. Three hours a week of that, at $30 an hour with taxes, is another $4,680 a year.

## What to do with this

Not cancel everything. Some of those tools earn their price. Do this instead:

1. **Pull last month's card statement** and highlight every software charge. Most owners find one they forgot.
2. **For each one, write what you actually use it for.** One line. If two tools have the same line, one of them goes.
3. **Multiply the rest by sixty.** That's five years. Decide if each is worth that.
4. **Count the re-typing.** Anywhere the same information goes in twice, there's a cost that never shows on a statement.

If the total still stings, that's when owning one system starts to make sense. The [calculator on my homepage](/#overhead) does this arithmetic with your numbers.
