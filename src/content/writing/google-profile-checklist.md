When someone searches for what you do nearby, the first thing they see usually isn't your website. It's your Google Business Profile: the box with your hours, photos, reviews and a call button. Plenty of people never click through to the site at all.

Most profiles I look at haven't been touched in years. Here's what to check.

## The basics

- **Hours, including holidays.** Wrong hours cost you a customer and a review.
- **Primary category.** Pick the most specific one that fits. It matters more than anything you write.
- **Description.** What you do, for whom, and where, in plain words.
- **Phone and website.** The website link should go to the most useful page, not always the homepage.

## What most profiles are missing

- **Services and products, with prices where you can.** Google uses them to match searches.
- **Recent photos.** Real ones: your work, your people, your place. A profile whose newest photo is four years old looks closed.
- **Posts.** A short update every couple of weeks tells Google and customers you're still here.
- **Answers to reviews.** All of them, good and bad. Short and specific.

## Getting reviews without being awkward

Ask at the moment the customer is happiest, usually right when the job's done. Make it one tap: a text with a direct link to your review form, or a card at the counter with a QR code. Businesses that ask every time get more reviews in a month than businesses that don't get in a year.

## No storefront?

You can still have a profile. Google calls it a service-area business. When you set it up, say you serve customers at their location, hide your address, and list the towns or counties you cover. You'll still appear on the map for searches in that area.

## And Bing

Bing has its own version, Bing Places, and it can import your Google profile in a few minutes. It also supplies results to Copilot and ChatGPT search, so it's worth the five minutes.

If you'd rather someone else did it, it's part of [websites and Google profiles](/help/websites/).
