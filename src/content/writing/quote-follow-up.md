Most small businesses send a quote and wait. If the customer doesn't reply, the owner assumes they went elsewhere. Often they didn't. They got busy, meant to reply, and forgot. So did you.

A follow-up isn't pushy. It's the difference between a quote that's lost and one that's just late.

## A routine that works

**Day 2: check it arrived.**

> Hi Sarah, just making sure the quote for the kitchen came through. Happy to answer anything.

Short. It also catches the quotes that went to spam, which is more of them than you'd think.

**Day 7: make it easy to say yes.**

> Hi Sarah, I've got openings the week of the 14th if you'd like to go ahead. Reply here or call me.

A date gives them something to decide about.

**Day 14: let them say no.**

> Hi Sarah, I'll close this out for now so I'm not filling your inbox. If you want to pick it back up, just reply.

This one gets more replies than the other two. People will tell you "we went with someone else" or "the budget isn't there until spring," and both are worth knowing.

Then stop.

## Make it not depend on memory

The routine fails the same way every time: the owner gets busy. The fix is to have it run on its own.

- **The simplest version** is a calendar reminder for each quote at day 2, 7 and 14.
- **Better** is a sheet with every open quote, the date it went out, and a column for the next follow-up, checked every morning.
- **Best** is when the messages go out automatically, in your words, from your number or email, and stop the moment the customer replies.

The last one is a single [automation](/help/automations/), usually connected to whatever you already quote in. It's also the place where a little AI can help, by reading a reply and telling you whether it's a yes, a no, or a question.

## Keep score

Track how many quotes you send each month and how many turn into jobs. If the follow-ups move that number even a few points, they pay for themselves many times over.
