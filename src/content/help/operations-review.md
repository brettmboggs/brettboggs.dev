I ran operations for ten years before I wrote software. A service lane, a car wash, then a group of them, then a district. The problems were almost never where the owner thought they were.

So the review starts with one real job. We follow it from the first phone call to the money landing in the bank, and I watch where it slows down. Then I ask whoever does the work to walk me through yesterday morning, step by step. The steps people skip because they're used to them are usually the ones that cost the most.

## What we look at

- **Where the same information gets typed twice.** A name and address that goes into a scheduling app, then an invoice, then the books.
- **Where things get dropped.** Quotes that never get a follow-up. Missed calls nobody returns. Reviews nobody asks for.
- **What you pay for and barely use.** Most businesses I see pay for three or four tools that overlap.
- **What only lives in someone's head.** The thing that breaks when one person is out sick. Often that person is the owner, which is its own kind of fix: [a business that runs without you](/help/runs-without-you/).

## What you get

A written plan, usually two or three pages. Each item says what it fixes, what it saves in hours or dollars, and what it costs to fix, including the option of doing nothing. It ranks them. The top one is usually cheap.

The savings are plain arithmetic you can check. Hours lost a week, times the people losing them, times what an hour costs you with taxes, times 52. Two people losing an hour a day at $30 is about $15,600 a year. A fix that won't pay for itself in a year or so goes to the bottom of the list.

Some of the plan will be for me to build. Some will be a setting in a tool you already pay for. I'll say which is which.

## Who it's for

Owners who know something is costing them time or money but can't say exactly what. The $350 review fits businesses up to about twenty people. Bigger companies, with several departments or locations, need more than one conversation, so I quote those after the free look. It's still credited toward the work. Service businesses, shops, makers, offices. You don't need to be good with computers. That's my job.
